# How much ground did armies occupy? Battle density, 1700–1815 (and antiquity), for sizing the game's maps

Research note, 2026-10-24, by a research agent. Each figure is tagged sourced or estimated; the sourced ones were not re-checked one by one.

**Question.** How much ground did armies of a given size occupy and fight over, and what square map does
the game need for 4–5k soldiers (a typical match) and for 32k (the extreme scenario, 16k a side), with room to
approach and manoeuvre?

**How to read the numbers.** Each figure is tagged:

- **[S]**: sourced. The source is named in the row or the sentence, and listed at the end.
- **[E]**: my estimate. It is either derived arithmetically from sourced figures (the working is shown), or
  measured roughly from standard battle maps (Esposito & Elting atlas, Chandler, the Wikipedia maps). Front
  lengths of whole battles are almost all [E]. Historians rarely state them, and when they do they
  disagree. Waterloo is given as "3 miles" by one source and "5 miles" by another, depending on whether
  Plancenoit counts.
- **[M]**: from memory of a standard work that I could not re-check online in this session. Verify against
  the book before quoting it in a project doc.

Strengths are the usual modern figures (mostly the Wikipedia infoboxes, which cite Chandler, Smith's *Data
Book* and others) and are rounded.

## 1. Frontage and depth of formations

### 1.1 The space one man takes

| Item | Value | Tag / source |
|---|---|---|
| Frontage per file, close order, elbows touching (French 1791 drill) | ~22 in ≈ **0.56 m** | [S] wargameds.com summary of the 1791 *Règlement* |
| Practical frontage per file in line, with the officers and NCOs on the flanks of each company | **0.60–0.65 m** | [E] (gives the sourced 156 m for 280 files, below) |
| French pace (*pas*) of the 1791 regulation | 2 French feet ≈ **0.65 m** | [S] Hamilton Papers note on the 1791 ordinance |
| Distance between ranks | ~1 pace (0.65 m) from back to breast; a 3-rank line is ~2–2.5 m deep | [E] |
| Mounted man, close order | **~1 m** of front (the British Horse Society width of horse and rider is ~1.5 m; troopers rode knee to knee, closer than that) | [E], with the [S] forum discussion of the BHS figure |

### 1.2 Infantry battalion

Field strengths were far below the paper strength, often by a third after a few weeks of campaigning.

| Army | Paper strength (organisation) | Typical field strength | Ranks | Line frontage at field strength | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| French (1808 onward) | 6 coys × 140 = 840 | 500–650 | 3 | **~110–140 m** (at full 840: **156 m / 171 yd**) | 156 m [S] (wargameds); the rest [E] |
| British | 10 coys, ~1,000 paper | 450–650 (Waterloo average ~550) | 2 | **~150–200 m** (two ranks double the frontage per man) | [E] |
| Austrian | 6 coys × ~180 = ~1,000+ | 600–900 | 3 | **~150–200 m** | [E] |
| Russian (1812) | 4 coys, ~700 paper | 400–550 | 3 (2 in practice late in the wars) | **~90–120 m** | [E] |
| Prussian (1813) | 4 coys, ~800 paper | 500–700 | 3 (the 3rd rank formed skirmishers) | **~110–150 m** | [E] |

Rule of thumb [E]: **a 3-rank line takes ~0.2 m of front per man (about 5 men per metre); a 2-rank line
takes ~0.3 m per man (about 3.3 men per metre).** A 600-man battalion is therefore ~120 m wide in three ranks
and ~180 m in two.

**Column.** The French column by division (*colonne de division*) had a front of two companies, ~40–60 m
[E], and was 3 divisions × 3 ranks deep. With the distances between divisions it was ~15–20 m deep closed up,
or ~40–60 m at half distance [E]. A late-18th-century manual works through an 800-man battalion in 8 divisions:
a division front of 35 paces (~23 m) [S] (e-rara, translated evolutions manual). Vimeiro's French column was
"about 40 files wide and 48 ranks deep" [S] (Wikipedia, Vimeiro). That is a brigade-sized mass about 25–30 m
wide and ~45 m deep [E].

**Square.** The usual square was four ranks deep on each face. A 600-man battalion makes faces of ~35–40
files, so **~25–35 m on a side, ~600–1,000 m² of ground** [E]. Wikipedia (*Napoleonic tactics*) gives 4–6
ranks [S].

**Interval between battalions.** I found no clean online quote from the 1791 *Règlement*. The documented
practice is a small interval in line, roughly 10–25 m [E], so that companies did not mix. Battalions moving in
column kept a **"deploying interval"** equal to the frontage the battalion would take in line (~100–150 m) so
that it could deploy without crowding its neighbour [E]. This is the standard description in Nosworthy and
Nafziger, cited from memory [M]. For the game: **lines nearly touch; columns stand one battalion-front apart.**

### 1.4 Brigade, division, corps

The standard deployment was in two lines, with a reserve behind. One translated period treatise gives
**300–400 paces (~200–260 m) between the first and second lines, and the reserve 200 paces (~130 m) behind
the second** [S] (e-rara treatise). Lines were spaced so that musket fire aimed at the first line did not hit the
second, yet the second could come up in a few minutes.

| Formation | Men | Frontage | Depth | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Brigade (4–6 battalions), one line, or two lines of 2–3 battalions | 2,500–4,000 | 300–600 m | 50 m in one line; ~300 m in two | [E] |
| Division (8–12 battalions + 1–2 batteries), two lines | 5,000–9,000 | **0.7–1.2 km** | **0.4–0.6 km** | [E] |
| Corps (2–4 divisions + light cavalry + reserve artillery), two lines + reserve | 15,000–30,000 | **1.5–3 km** | **1–1.5 km** (cavalry, park and trains further back) | [E] |
| Army of ~100,000 (Napoleonic) | 100,000 | **8 km** | **2.5 km** | [M] Dupuy, *The Evolution of Weapons and Warfare* (1980), dispersion table: 20 km² per 100,000 men, ~5,000 men/km², ~200 m² per man |

Wellington at Waterloo: about 68,000 men on a ridge roughly 4 km long, deployed "in depth, especially on his
right and centre" [S] (Bell of Lost Souls history piece). That is **~17,000 men per km of front for one side**
[E], about the densest of the period.

**Per-side density** [E]: **~8,000–17,000 men per km of front** for a deliberately formed army, with
Dupuy's 12,400/km [M] in the middle. **Area density: ~4,000–6,000 men/km²** over the ground the army actually
stands on.

Waterloo is the extreme: on the Anglo-allied front alone, ~140,000 men on 4 km is **35,000/km**. Chandler,
as relayed by a forum post, gives 28,000 per mile for the Allies and 14,500 per mile for the French on a
5-mile front [S, second hand].

**Pattern** [E]:

- **Concentrated, frontal battles** (Albuera, Talavera, Friedland, Auerstedt, Borodino, Waterloo):
  **20,000–35,000 men per km of front (both sides), ~5,000–10,000 men/km².**
- **Manoeuvre battles over wide fields** (Austerlitz, Jena, Wagram, Leipzig): **11,000–17,000 per km,
  ~2,000–2,600 men/km².** These are the battles where the decisive acts were flank marches and converging
  corps, which is what the game wants to allow.
- **Small battles of 10–35k** (Maida, Vimeiro, Corunna): **~7,000–11,000 per km, ~5,000 men/km²** on a fight
  area of **2–6 km²**.

**Approach pattern** [E]: on the morning of a battle the armies were typically **2–8 km apart**. Corps that
decided the day marched in from **15–30 km** (one day's ordinary march) and sometimes from **50–110 km**
(a forced march over two days). Napoleon's *bataillon carré* kept corps "within a day's march of each other"
[S] (napoleonguide.com), roughly 20–30 km [E].

**March rates** [E, standard figures]: the French ordinary step (76 paces a minute × 0.65 m) is ~3 km/h; on a
road with halts, an infantry column covered ~4 km/h, and 20–25 km made a normal day's march, 35–50 km a
forced march. Cavalry walked at 6–7 km/h and trotted at 12–15 km/h. A corps of 25,000 on one road stretched
over ~15–25 km with its artillery and trains, which is why corps used several parallel roads.

## 4. Vision and scouting

**What a commander could see** (Wellington's telescope, Waterloo 200 / Royal Armouries) [S]:

| Distance | What can be made out |
|---|---|
| 300 m | Flag designs, hence which unit |
| 450 m | Individual figures, uniform colours |
| 600 m | Groups of files (formations) |
| 800 m | Movements that suggest intentions |
| 1,200 m | Guns; infantry formations, and from their order the quality of the troops |
| 1,500 m | Infantry line as a solid black line; cavalry as a notched black line |
| 4,000 m | Main features of houses |
| 8,000 m | Troops massing, shown by darker hillsides |
| 10,000 m | Windmills and towers |

Kevin Zucker (wargame designer) gives similar figures: a battalion is easy to see at 1,500 yd with the naked
eye, infantry and cavalry are distinct at ~1,200 m, and an 800-man battalion is still a dark mass at 2,000 m
[S] (OSG blog). French tests of the 1850s, as relayed by Nosworthy, are similar for cavalry: its pace, direction
and uniform colour were readable at ~650 yd [S, second hand].

Limits [E]: **powder smoke** hid a fighting line within minutes on a still day; **ground** (reverse slopes,
woods, villages, standing crops) hid whole corps, as with Wellington's reverse slope; morning **fog** hid
the field at Austerlitz and Auerstedt (where it is attested at Poppel [S]). Practically, **a commander on a
height read the battle within ~3–5 km and saw large movements to ~8 km on a clear day.**

**The cavalry screen** [E]: light cavalry patrolled **one day's march (20–40 km)** ahead and on the flanks
of the marching corps. Murat's reserve cavalry led the 1806 advance through the Thuringian Forest [S]
(napoleonguide, the 1806 accounts), but no source I found gives a figure for the depth of the screen.

**Reliable reports** [E]: a galloper with relays made ~10–15 km/h, so a report from 20 km away was 1.5–2 h
old on arrival. Within ~10 km, reports were timely enough to act on the same day. Beyond 30 km, they were
history.

**How armies lost contact** (all attested in the standard accounts) [E summary]:

- Grouchy lost Blücher after Ligny and marched to Wavre, ~20 km from the decisive field.
- Napoleon at Jena misjudged which Prussian army he faced.
- Napoleon at Marengo believed the Austrians were escaping and dispersed his army.
- Washington at Brandywine had a report of Cornwallis's flanking march but discounted it.

A "known terrain, fog on units" game models this well: the armies knew the roads but not who was on them.

(10,000–15,000 per side), but were **15–20× shallower**, so the ground actually covered by troops was 10–20×
denser. Ancient maps can be smaller in depth but not much narrower.

## 6. For the game

### 6.1 Density at 1:1 scale

The game draws one figure per soldier, so **no scaling of men is needed: historical densities apply as they
are.** That also means **formation frontages must stay at real scale**. A 600-man French battalion is ~120 m
wide whatever the map size. That fact drives the advice in 6.3.

Working densities [E], from §2:

- in the fight zone: **~5,000 men/km²** (the small battles and Dupuy), i.e. **~200 m² per man**;
- front: **~8,000–11,000 men per km for both sides together** in small battles.

### 6.2 Ground needed

| Scenario | Front at historical density | Fight zone | + manoeuvre room (the flanks: ×1.5–2 in width) | + approach (the armies start out of sight, 1.5–3 km from the fight zone each side) | Map side |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **4–5k total** (2–2.5k a side: ~3–4 battalions, 1–2 squadrons, 1 battery each) | **0.5–1 km** (Maida: 11k on 1.5 km) | ~1 × 1 km | ~2 km wide | +1.5 km each side → **~4–5 km** | **4–6 km** |
| **32k total** (16k a side: a strong division to a small corps each) | **~3 km** (Vimeiro, Corunna: 31–33k on ~3 km) | ~3 × 2 km | ~5–6 km wide (room for a Brandywine or Saalfeld flank march) | +2.5–3 km each side → **~9–11 km** | **10–12 km** |

Check against real time [E]:

- A **4 km** map is ~1 h of ordinary infantry march edge to edge and ~25 min at a cavalry trot. That is the
  length of a long RTS match, so approach and fight both fit.
- A **12 km** map with the armies starting ~6–8 km apart gives 1.5–2 h of real-time infantry approach, the
  same order as Davout's or Desaix's last leg. That is good for a long historical scenario. In a match, it
  needs a time speed-up during the approach.
- A **16 km** map holds 32k at an average of ~125 men/km². It is right only for **multi-axis scenarios** (a
  converging corps arriving from a map edge, an Auerstedt or Waterloo in miniature) and with time
  acceleration. For 4–5k, 16 km is empty ground: the armies might never meet.

**Recommendation** [E]:

- **Scale the map side with the army size.** As a formula: map side ≈ 2 × approach + √(N / ρ) × 1.5, with
  ρ ≈ 4,000–5,000 men/km² and an approach of 1.5–3 km. This gives about **4 km for 4–5k** and about
  **10–12 km for 32k**.
- Keep **16 km** as the ceiling, for 32k scenarios with off-map or edge arrivals and time acceleration.
- If a single size must serve every match, use **8 km**. It holds 32k at Jena or Austerlitz density, though
  that is tight for a full approach, and it leaves 4–5k room to search for each other under fog. At 4–5k,
  though, an hour can pass without contact unless time accelerates.

### 6.3 Compression: distance or time?

**Compressing distance** (an 8 km historical field drawn on a 4 km map, factor c = 2):

- March times fall by c, which is good for pacing.
- Unless heights are also scaled by 1/c, **slopes steepen by c**. A gentle 5% glacis becomes 10%, a 10%
  slope becomes 20%: cavalry and artillery slopes cross gameplay thresholds, and lines of sight and reverse
  slopes shrink.
- **Units do not shrink**, because the game draws one figure per soldier. A 120 m battalion now covers twice
  the share of the terrain, so a ridge holds half as many battalions and the density per km² rises by c²
  (4×). The flanks close in, and manoeuvre room disappears first.
- **Weapon ranges stay real** (musket ~100–200 m, artillery 600–1,200 m). Relative to the map, guns reach c
  times further, so a central battery would command most of a compressed map.
- **Sight ranges** (§4) also stay real, so the fog of war covers a smaller share of the map, and scouting
  matters less.

**Compressing time** (true 1:1 ground, a game clock that runs faster, e.g. ×2–×4 during the approach and
×1 in contact):

- This keeps formations, slopes, ranges and sight consistent with the 1:1 figures, and it is the usual answer
  in historical wargames.
- The costs: fatigue, reload and morale timings must stay readable at speed, and the speed should probably
  drop automatically when enemies are spotted.

**Advice** [E]: **keep the ground at 1:1 and compress time.** If a compression of distance is unavoidable
for a scenario, keep it **≤ 1.5×**, scale heights by the same factor so the slopes stay true, and accept that
fields will feel crowded.
