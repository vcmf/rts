# Fix the claim grid's leader, gates and rebuild

The claim grid's four weak points each have a known fix, and none of them requires replacing the grid. The three-battalion stall comes from two problems plus a misrouted failure. Three goals that overlap cannot all be reached in any order. The per-tick keyed visit order rotates leadership, so every formation nibbles a halved step and all of them creep into contact together. Then a block by a *formation* falls into the *terrain* failure path and drops orders, the same regression OpenRA shipped and had to fix ([PR #22487](https://github.com/OpenRA/OpenRA/pull/22487)). Shipped engines treat goal contention as arrival ("blocked by a settled friend at my goal counts as arrived", with an arrival radius that grows with frustration). Real-time multi-agent path finding treats leader churn with a priority that ages and is kept until the formation makes progress (PIBT, wait-die). Acts that cannot be cut short should be checked once at order time against a reserved swept region and refused, or swapped for the next shape that fits. Tabletop rules ([Rank & File 2nd](https://crusaderpublishing.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2nd-rf-free-pdf-29-nov-2025.pdf)) and robotics ([Alonso-Mora et al. 2017](https://people.csail.mit.edu/jalonsom/docs/17-alonsomora-IJRR.pdf)) do exactly this, and that reservation is what makes a mid-change snap safe. In melee, collision should only clamp. Combat should move the contact line in small per-tick steps, the winner following within the clamp and the loser's blocked rear deciding between pushing friends, taking cohesion loss, or routing. The cost overrun is almost all per-man rebuilding: profiling the prototype shows the grid queries cost about 0.12 ms a tick, while placing 400 men and stamping 9 cells each, twice per formation per tick, costs about 2.6 ms. Two exact fixes bring 4.64 ms down to 3.06 ms, and company- or bin-rectangle claims should bring the formation step to about 2.0–2.2 ms. That last figure is an estimate, not a measurement.

**Reading this report.** A claim followed by a link comes from that source. "Measured" means a profile or benchmark the research team ran on an Apple M1 Pro in Node 22 against the prototype at commit `1df4008` (`src/sim/claims.ts`, scenario X-14). A low-end laptop is probably 1.5–3x slower, which is itself an estimate. "Design" or "inference" marks a recommendation the sources support but do not state. The earlier report *Formation collision and command chaining* recommended min-clamps over a snapshot, a formed/loose state table, and suspending orders rather than clearing them. This report keeps all three and narrows them to the claim grid.

## Three converging battalions stall from goal contention and a rotating leader

### What the prototype actually does wrong

The stall has three causes, and each needs its own fix. **Goal over-subscription** is the first. When three battalions are sent to overlapping ground, the room for all three does not exist, so no visit order can solve it. **Order churn** is the second. Each tick draws a new keyed order, so each formation leads about a third of the time, takes a halved step when it does, and drifts into margin contact with the others. **Misrouting** is the third. Contact with a formation is sent down the "stalled on impassable ground" path, which drops orders after 1 s. Two smaller defects in the code make jams more likely, both read from the code rather than tested. If the overlap test checks the new core plus margin against the other formation's core plus margin, the real standoff is two margins (4 m), not one. And at junctions where three or more formations claim a cell, the two-slot cell's `more` flag reports the cell as claimed *even to its own owners*, so X-06's three-on-one-gap case can block everyone, including the formations that hold the cell. Both should be checked on X-06 before any jam logic is tuned.

### Shipped engines treat goal contention as arrival

Every engine studied keeps "blocked by terrain" and "blocked by a movable friend" as separate states with separate responses. OpenRA's blocker classes (`None, Immovable, Stationary, All`) make this explicit ([TraitsInterfaces.cs](https://github.com/OpenRA/OpenRA/blob/bleed/OpenRA.Mods.Common/TraitsInterfaces.cs)). Its documented failure is this project's bug. After a retry cooldown landed, "the move would abort when the unit got blocked and we'd try again after a ~1 second cooldown", fresh orders no longer checked whether they were blocking others, and three harvesters "sit in place and deadlock". The fix kept the order alive and skipped the cooldown while the unit was blocking someone ([PR #22487](https://github.com/OpenRA/OpenRA/pull/22487)).

For contention at a shared goal, three engines settle it with geometry, not priority. Recoil's ground movement ends a move with `TriggerCallArrived()` when the unit it hits is idle and touches the goal, a rule the code describes as killing "long pushing contests". It also patched the exact three-battalion geometry: "several large units can end up surrounding a waypoint and block each other from touching it, because it sits diagonally from all of them", which it fixed by scaling the arrival radius by 1.45 ([GroundMoveType.cpp](https://github.com/beyond-all-reason/RecoilEngine/blob/master/rts/Sim/MoveTypes/GroundMoveType.cpp)). Recoil's arrival radius also grows with a leaky frustration counter: `(goalRadius + extraRadius) × (numIdlingSlowUpdates + 1)`, where the counter goes +1 per idle slow update and −1 per moving one, capped at 16 (same source). Stratagus widens the acceptance range by one cell on each failure (`this->Range++`), so converging units settle in rings ([action_move.cpp](https://github.com/Wargus/stratagus/blob/master/src/action/action_move.cpp)). Brood War, as reimplemented by OpenBW, ends shared-goal contention by having the late arriver call `set_unit_immovable` on itself when the blocker overlaps its goal and either the blocker has a frustration counter of at least 30 or the two goals overlap ([bwgame.h](https://github.com/OpenBW/openbw/blob/master/bwgame.h)). Brood War's state machine also steps at half and then quarter velocity before entering collision handling, the same halving the prototype uses. It then tries a tangential slide before it waits or repaths, and it reads the *blocker's* frustration as well as its own. If the blocker has been stuck a long time, waiting is pointless. If it has just started moving, waiting is right.

### Priority has to age and persist, or the jam rotates its holder

The MAPF literature explains why the per-tick redraw fails. Silver's WHCA\* lets agents "step aside and allow others to pass" by giving "every agent … the highest priority for a short period of time". That holds priority per window, not per step, and cut failures in a 100-agent bottleneck from about 20% to under 2% ([Silver 2005](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/18726/18726-52-22369-1-10-20210928.pdf)). PIBT adds one to an agent's priority each timestep it is not at its goal, resets it on arrival, breaks ties with a distinct per-agent value, and lets a blocked high-priority agent lend its priority to the agent in its way. If that agent cannot move, it backtracks ([Okumura et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.11282)). Database deadlock prevention adds the same anti-starvation rule: a transaction that loses keeps its original timestamp, so it eventually becomes the oldest. That point comes from textbook knowledge of Rosenkrantz et al. 1978, not from a fetched copy. Pottinger, writing about the Age of Empires movement code, warned that without priority "you're likely to see units doing a merry-go-round dance as each demands that the other move out of its way". He also said a resolved collision must be remembered, or units reverse their own decisions ([Coordinated Unit Movement](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/coordinated-unit-movement)).

Simultaneous adjudication shows that the prototype's symptom is what order-independent rules produce by default. In Diplomacy, equal strengths contending for one province all bounce ([DATC v3.0](https://webdiplomacy.net/doc/DATC_v3_0.html)). Pedestrian cellular automata with parallel update add a "friction" probability that all conflicting moves are denied, and that denial produces clogging and arching ([Kirchner et al. 2003](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0209383)). Halving everyone's step is a partial bounce. What is needed is an order-independent way to pick *one* winner. A total order over state, such as frustration first and then a keyed hash of `(seed, window, id)`, picks one winner deterministically and does not depend on list order. PIBT is order-independent for the same reason.

The geometry of the bodies settles how to frame conflicts. Li et al. show that one overlap between large agents produces many grid conflicts, and that resolving them one cell at a time is up to three orders of magnitude slower than resolving them as a block ([MC-CBS, AAAI 2019](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/4756)). A battalion on 2 m cells is a large agent of 15×5 to 80×10 cells. The grid should therefore detect contacts and the *formation pair* should be the unit of decision. Full MAPF solvers (CBS, Push and Swap, Push and Rotate) solve an offline problem, getting everyone to an exact goal. That is the wrong tool for a 20 Hz sim driven by player orders. The parts that transfer are aging priority, inheritance with a depth cap, rotation-free resolution (PIBT⊗), and Silver's rule that an agent sitting on its goal still cooperates.

### Rules the evidence supports

The prototype's rule that freed cells stay claimed until the next tick should stay. In Stern et al.'s conflict taxonomy, forbidding *following* conflicts also forbids cycles and swaps ([Stern et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08291)). At 20 Hz the step is far smaller than a cell, so the one-tick lag is invisible. The rest of the jam handling becomes a propose-and-grant resolver, which is design built from the sources above.

| Rule | Content | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Propose from a snapshot | Each formation lists candidates from frozen start-of-tick occupancy: full step, S/2, S/4, a lateral slide toward the freer side, wait | Brood War halving and `SlideFree`; Pottinger case 3.2 "avoid if possible"; Recoil phased update |
| Stable leader | Grant in descending π = (frustration, hash(seed, ⌊tick/40⌋, id)), replacing the per-tick redraw; the hash changes every 2 s window, not every tick | WHCA\* windowed priority; PIBT aging; wait-die keeps the timestamp |
| Progress, not motion | Frustration +1 per denied tick, −1 per 4 ticks of progress toward the waypoint; a lateral slide does not count as progress | Recoil leaky counter; Brood War's fast reset to 2 is a known flip-flop risk |
| Pairwise decision | Conflicts are recorded and resolved per formation pair, not per cell | MC-CBS |
| Goal contention settles | If the chain of blockers ends at a halted or arrived formation and I am within T of my goal, arrive where I stand and keep my facing and orders. T = T0·(1 + frustration/20) | Recoil arrival, Stratagus `Range++`, Brood War `set_unit_immovable` |
| Deadlock cycle | Build a wait-for graph from denied candidates. In a strongly connected group of movers, grant the top-π member; the others wait or lend it priority. Rotations are forbidden for rigid bodies | Coffman (wait-for cycles); PIBT⊗; Diplomacy cycle rules |
| Inheritance, depth ≤ 2 | If my best candidate is blocked by one lower-π formation not yet granted, run it first with my priority, restricted to moving out of my swept area | PIBT |
| Escalation ladder | Wait (≤ 10 ticks, longer if the blocker is progressing away) → slide → ask the blocker to yield → local repath treating progressing friends as passable at a cost → settle or report | OpenRA `CellIsEvacuating`; Stratagus moving-unit cost 5; [OpenRA #20972](https://github.com/OpenRA/OpenRA/issues/20972) |
| Never drop on a friend block | `Halted-Blocked` keeps the order and reports "halted: blocked by 2nd Bn". Only terrain or immovable blocks reach the stalled path, and that path reports rather than cancelling | OpenRA #22487; AoE II DE "stuck Villagers … should always show up as idle now" ([update 153015](https://www.ageofempires.com/news/age-of-empires-ii-definitive-edition-update-153015/)) |
| Hysteresis | Enter halted at frustration ≥ 20 (1 s). Leave when a full step has been grantable for 5 ticks or the blocker's state changes | Brood War minimum dwell of 7 frames and thresholds at 20/25/30 |

The goal-contention rule fixes the three-battalion case on its own. The stable-leader rule stops creeping contact when goals do *not* overlap. OpenRA's experience supplies two cautions. A repath that treats every unit as a wall picks detours "100x worse" around a busy choke ([#20972](https://github.com/OpenRA/OpenRA/issues/20972)). And random nudges of large bodies are big visible moves, so a slide should be bounded to a few cells and applied only along the contact tangent. Brood War's counterexample is also useful. Blizzard's fix for the worst worker jams was to switch off collision between harvesters in certain states ([Wyatt, codeofhonor](https://www.codeofhonor.com/blog/the-starcraft-path-finding-hack)). Friendly columns on road march are a candidate for the same exemption, which fits the earlier report's "loose" state.

## Acts that cannot be halved should reserve their swept area or be refused

### Tabletop rules prevent overlap before it can happen

The surveyed tabletop rules never let an indivisible act end in overlap, and they achieve that by refusing the act. Rank & File 2nd states it plainly: "If there is not enough space for the new formation you cannot perform the change. Units are not allowed to end a formation change on top of another unit, even if they will subsequently move off with their remaining half movement allowance." All changes are made "on the centre front of unit with the new formation remaining facing in the same direction". An about-face "changes the facing of the stands in the unit by 180 degrees without changing the relative position of those stands" ([R&F 2nd](https://crusaderpublishing.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2nd-rf-free-pdf-29-nov-2025.pdf)). General d'Armée shows the failed-act alternative. On a failed formation-change roll, "the formation change has not been completed": the unit keeps its old formation, unformed, and "still maintains all the benefits of its formation" ([GdA FAQ](https://loughtonstrikeforce.co.uk/gda/faq/)). The designer's note in R&F gives the period logic: "There is a reason historical formations maintained sufficient spacing to manoeuvre."

Computer games went the other way and refuse nothing. 0 A.D. computes new slot offsets around the current centre from footprints and member count alone, with no fit test against obstacles or other formations, and each man walks to his slot under his own pathing ([Formation.js](https://github.com/0ad/0ad/blob/master/binaries/data/mods/public/simulation/components/Formation.js)). Its formation controller does not block movement or pathfinding, so a formation reserves no space at all ([template_formation.xml](https://github.com/0ad/0ad/blob/master/binaries/data/mods/public/simulation/templates/template_formation.xml)). Its turns past `MaxTurningAngle` are full re-formations, not rigid rotations (same source). Total War forms a square progressively, and the unit is weak until it is complete ([Empire TW fandom](https://empiretotalwar.fandom.com/wiki/Square_Formation), community source). No source was found for any Total War title refusing a change for lack of room. These engines tolerate transient overlap between men, and this project's invariant (formed bodies never overlap) does not.

### Robotics turns the refusal into a guarantee

Alonso-Mora, Baker and Rus accept a new formation only if it fits inside a convex obstacle-free region that "contains all the robots at their current positions". Because the region is convex, this "guarantees that the transition to the new formation will be collision-free". They optimise each fixed template (square, line, T) inside the region and take the cheapest feasible one. For a rigid carried object, they require that every one of K interpolated poses lies inside. When nothing fits, robots move individually and "the formation is likely broken during the transition" ([IJRR 2017](https://people.csail.mit.edu/jalonsom/docs/17-alonsomora-IJRR.pdf)). Silver's reservation table supplies the bookkeeping: entries "are considered impassable" by later planners, and the table "must be capable of marking off any occupied region" for agents of varying size ([Silver 2005](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/18726/18726-52-22369-1-10-20210928.pdf)).

### Concrete rules for each act

These rules are design that combines the two sources. A **formation change** (30–100 s, companies sweeping on timed paths) is checked once at order time. Its claim is the union of each company's footprint sampled along its timed path, which `changeWet` already samples. The convex hull of the current and target footprints is the cheaper conservative stand-in, taken from Alonso-Mora's gate. If the claim meets another formed body's cells or claim, the order is refused with a reason, as in R&F 12.7. Alternatively, the order is offered the next template in a preference list that does fit, as in Alonso-Mora's argmin over templates. While the change runs, other formations treat the claim as occupied. Each company's share is released as the company arrives, or the whole claim is released at the end. The claim also suits the grid's cost model: it is one reserved region per act, which is far cheaper than re-gating 400 men on each sample.

**Face about** should not move anything. Following R&F, the companies' rectangles stay where they are, and only the facing, the rank order and the front-centre anchor change. The anchor jump to the old rear edge is bookkeeping, not motion, so the act needs no collision check. If the companies visibly counter-march, the footprint is still static, and the claim is simply the current footprint.

A **break mid-change** that snaps to a shape is safe by construction if the active claim covers both shapes and every pose between them. The snap then lands inside space already reserved. R&F 17.9 gives a tested way to pick the target. A unit in square or march column forced into a compulsory move changes "in order of preference" to attack column, double line or line, does so after the move, and "will fall back as far as required to clear all friends, even if this is more than its compulsory result". GdA's alternative is to keep the old shape, degraded. Either fits the claim model. The snap needs hysteresis, and 0 A.D. supplies the precedent: it disables centre movement on a mid-move shape switch "so we can't get stuck in a loop of switching shape causing center to change causing shape to switch back" ([Formation.js](https://github.com/0ad/0ad/blob/master/binaries/data/mods/public/simulation/components/Formation.js)).

A **rally that might form inside a friend** is prevented one step earlier in every tabletop set. R&F's compulsory moves "must pass beyond [a friend] to the first available space", and a rallying unit "will halt on the spot … in its current formation" and may only change facing. A Close Run Thing eliminates a retreating melee loser whose path is blocked ([CRT rules](https://www.tabletopgaming.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Close-run-thing-Napoleonic-core-rules.pdf-·-version-1.pdf)). For this sim, a routed body is loose, holds no formed claim, and passes through friends at a cohesion cost to them, as the earlier report recommended and as R&F's elan test for interpenetrated units implies. The unit may rally only where its formed footprint fits. If it does not fit, it keeps fleeing to the first free placement, or it runs a deterministic ring search for the nearest free anchor and walks there loose. 0 A.D.'s `PickSpawnPoint` is the precedent for the search: rings outward from the footprint, trying points "in alternating directions, starting from the front" ([CCmpFootprint.cpp](https://github.com/0ad/0ad/blob/master/source/simulation2/components/CCmpFootprint.cpp)).

The **form-square reaction** under a charge should follow the same rule as any formation change, with a timing test layered on top. R&F requires an elan test, and a pass gives a square marked disordered ([R&F 2nd](https://crusaderpublishing.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2nd-rf-free-pdf-29-nov-2025.pdf)). CRT allows an emergency square only if the charger started more than half its charge distance away. A square refused for lack of room therefore receives the charge in its current formation, which is a legible and historically defensible result.

## Combat moves the contact line in small steps and collision only clamps it

### Three tested models of giving ground

Tabletop rules agree that formed bodies do not shove continuously. Contact is a state, and its result is a discrete outcome move. In R&F, cavalry and skirmishers that lose "fall back" facing the enemy and disordered, and everyone else retreats facing away. Foot move 1D6+9" and mounted 2D6+9", "directly away from that enemy", and the unit routs if no legal path exists. The winner "may now move to occupy the ground … so long as this does not bring them to within 1" of any enemy", up to 3" ([R&F 2nd](https://crusaderpublishing.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2nd-rf-free-pdf-29-nov-2025.pdf)). CRT lets the loser withdraw a full move in march column facing away, or half a move facing the attacker in its current formation, and the winner may occupy the ground ([CRT](https://www.tabletopgaming.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Close-run-thing-Napoleonic-core-rules.pdf-·-version-1.pdf)). R&F's design note matches the history in the earlier report: "One side inevitably gives way before [crossing sabres] happens."

Computer games show the cost of each discrete option. Field of Glory II pushes the loser back a square and lets the winner follow, and its designer concedes that "a whole square is too far for a realistic pushback". The fixes he and players proposed include pushing back only every other time, decaying the odds of repeated pushbacks, limiting follow-ups, and keeping units in contact without moving. He preferred the last because it "would avoid altering the dynamic between the various troop-types" ([Slitherine forum](https://forum.slitherine.com/viewtopic.php?p=747481)). Players complained of "bizarre near-instantaneous penetrations" and of cheap units used to block pushbacks (same thread). Most Total War titles have no line-level push: "Troops aren't pushed by a superior enemy … they don't lose position (or win it)" ([Rome II discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/214950/discussions/0/682990896046710931/), community source). Total War: Pharaoh added stances. Advance "will push other units back", Hold digs in, and Give Ground lets units "retreat and slowly cede ground while maintaining formation and facing forward" ([Wargamer](https://www.wargamer.com/total-war-pharaoh/total-war-pharaoh-series-development-fans); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_War:_Pharaoh)). How the push is computed is not published. Grand Tactician resolves friendly overlap by rank: "the 'lower ranking' unit will slow down to let the 'higher ranking' unit move to its position" ([GT Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/654890/discussions/4/3094509155620731280)).

### The blocked loser is where rulesets differ

The hard case is a loser that cannot retreat because something stands behind it. Rulesets answer it in four different ways.

| Ruleset | Loser's retreat blocked by a friend | Blocked by enemy or impassable ground |
|---|---|---|
| Rank & File 2nd | Passes beyond to the first free space; the friend tests for disorder | Routs, or is removed if the terrain cannot be skirted |
| A Close Run Thing | Eliminated if no other path | Eliminated |
| DBA family | Pushes back friends facing the same way; otherwise destroyed (rule text quoted on [Fanaticus](https://fanaticus.boards.net/thread/478/when-ellies-recoil)) | Destroyed |
| General d'Armée | May fall back on supporting close-order friends, who may reform next phase ([GdA FAQ](https://loughtonstrikeforce.co.uk/gda/faq/)) | Not found |

### A rate-based clash that keeps the invariant

The design below follows from the sources but has not been tested by any of them. When neither side yields before contact, the touching bins halt at a shared contact line under the min-clamp from the earlier report. Each tick, the combat result sets a give-ground rate, and the loser's contact bins step back by d. The value of d is well under a company's depth and much smaller than FoG2's square, and it can be gated by stance as in Pharaoh. The winner's bins may follow by at most d, still clamped, so contact holds and the bodies never interpenetrate. This is R&F's "occupy ground" spread over time, and it fits FoG2's designer's preference for contact that persists without big jumps. The loser's backward step is an ordinary claim on the grid and goes through the same resolver as any move:

1. If the cells behind are free, the step is granted.
2. If a formed friend facing the same way holds them, either push that friend by d (DBA, recursive with a depth cap of 2, which matches the inheritance cap above) or deny the step and convert the shortfall into cohesion loss. The second option is FoG2's "stay in contact", and it is how "pressed back on the second line" emerges without shoving.
3. If an enemy or impassable ground holds them, escalate to rout or destruction (R&F 16.17, CRT, DBA).

Shapes bend because bins step independently. Only the bins along the engaged segment receive the combat step, and their neighbours follow up to a shear limit between adjacent bins. A line meeting a column at one point therefore bows, and a line meeting a line across its full width gives ground evenly. This reuses the terrain-lag mechanism the earlier report recommended, with the anchor slowing when the lag spread passes a threshold, following [Henry et al.](https://www.hubertshum.com/publications/tvcg2014formation/files/tvcg2014formation.pdf). R&F's wheel rule, that the inside corner stays in place and units may not "sideslip" into a better position, argues for a cap on lateral shear. Past that cap the battalion becomes disordered instead of bending further. A win by cavalry over infantry turns the infantry loose (a rout), and only then may the winner ride over the ground, so the formed-never-overlap invariant holds exactly. In 0 A.D., a pushed unit with high "pressure" is marked obstructed so that it "stop[s] earlier in many situations in a realistic-ish manner" ([CCmpUnitMotion_System.cpp](https://github.com/0ad/0ad/blob/master/source/simulation2/components/CCmpUnitMotion_System.cpp)). That supports making the give-ground rate decay as the loser compresses against friends.

Three parameters decide whether this reads well, and no source fixes them: d per tick, the shear cap between bins, and the cohesion charged per denied step. FoG2's experience says to err small on d.

## The 3 ms overrun is per-man rebuilding, not the grid

### Where the time goes

Profiling the prototype on X-14 (forty battalions crossing on a 301² grid, 1,200 ticks) located the cost. The function `claimFormation` takes about **53% of the tick**. It runs twice per formation per tick, once in `buildClaims` at the start of the tick and again after each formation steps. Each run places all 400 men with `placeAtSlot` and writes a 3×3 halo per man through the two-slot `claim()`. Grid queries (`blockedAt` and `claimedAt`) cost about **0.12 ms a tick**. Clearing three arrays of 90k cells costs about 3 µs. The quick rejects lost because `claimsNear` finds nearly every formation "near" someone cost about **0.45 ms**. Of the roughly 3.1 ms the grid adds, about 2.6 ms is rebuilding and about 0.5 ms is queries plus the lost rejects.

| Variant (X-14, measured) | ms/tick |
|---|---|
| Claims off | 1.55 |
| Claims on, as committed | **4.64** |
| + `claim()` unrolled, box held in locals | 4.31 |
| + stamp the halo once per distinct core cell (epoch-stamped `Uint32Array`), hash-identical | **3.46** |
| + at tick start, re-stamp each formation's post-step core-cell list from last tick | **3.06** |

The dedupe is exact, because men stand 0.6–1 m apart, so 4–6 share each 2 m core cell, and a repeated claim is a no-op. The reuse gives the same world hash at 30 s and 60 s on X-14, but casualties between ticks make it over-claim a fallen man's cell for one tick. That is safe but not hash-identical in a fight. Re-placing only the formations the casualty step flags would remove the difference.

### Shipped engines rasterise shapes incrementally

None of the three open engines studied clears and rebuilds occupancy from every unit every frame. OpenRA's `SetLocation` returns early when the cells are unchanged, and only otherwise removes and re-adds influence. A moving actor occupies both its from-cell and its to-cell, which is the prototype's held-cells rule ([Mobile.cs](https://github.com/OpenRA/OpenRA/blob/bleed/OpenRA.Mods.Common/Traits/Mobile.cs); [ActorMap.cs](https://github.com/OpenRA/OpenRA/blob/bleed/OpenRA.Mods.Common/Traits/World/ActorMap.cs)). 0 A.D. tests shapes against shapes, rasterises an oriented rectangle as one span per row (`RasterizeRectWithClearance`), and redraws only dirty shapes ([Rasterize.cpp](https://github.com/0ad/0ad/blob/master/source/simulation2/helpers/Rasterize.cpp); [CCmpObstructionManager.cpp](https://github.com/0ad/0ad/blob/master/source/simulation2/components/CCmpObstructionManager.cpp)). Its pushing grid clears only the cells touched that turn ([CCmpUnitMotion_System.cpp](https://github.com/0ad/0ad/blob/master/source/simulation2/components/CCmpUnitMotion_System.cpp)). Recoil's quad field skips units whose quad set is unchanged, dedupes queries with a per-query stamp (`tempNum`), keeps a coarse per-allyteam mask that can give false positives but never false negatives, and stores 8 inline owners per cell plus an overflow pool ([QuadField.cpp](https://github.com/beyond-all-reason/RecoilEngine/blob/master/rts/Sim/Misc/QuadField.cpp); [GroundBlockingObjectMap.h](https://github.com/beyond-all-reason/RecoilEngine/blob/master/rts/Sim/Misc/GroundBlockingObjectMap.h)). None of them publishes timings.

### Measured primitive costs point to rectangle claims

A synthetic benchmark with the prototype's shapes (40 formations × 8 companies × 3 ranks × 17 files) measured rasterising **320 oriented company rectangles with a 2 m margin at about 0.085 ms**. The per-man 3×3 stamp cost 0.24 ms with a simple write and 0.6–1.0 ms through the prototype's `claim()`. Rectangles write about 11x fewer cells, and most of their remaining cost is span set-up. The raster should be the overestimated conservative kind, covering every cell the rectangle touches, which is the GPU Gems definition ([Hasselgren et al. 2005](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-v-image-oriented-computing/chapter-42-conservative-rasterization)). It should also use the prototype's cell rule (`Math.round((x − x0)/cell)`) in both writer and reader. The raster uses only `+ − × ÷`, min/max and floor, so it is safe under the sim's float64 rules. Short spans of 3–12 cells are faster written with a plain loop than with `TypedArray.fill(v, a, b)` (0.085 vs 0.12 ms, measured). `fill` should be kept for whole-grid clears. Whole-grid clears stay negligible below about 1500² (≤ 0.04 ms for 16-bit) and should become sparse or epoch-based at 3000², where the arrays reach about 45 MB.

There is one caveat on fit. X-14's columns are snake-shaped along the trail, and their bins lag. Rectangles per company are exact only for straight shapes, so columns need one rectangle per bin (the sim already keeps per-bin frames) or a company rectangle widened by the lag spread. Changes in progress need per-company rectangles at the sampled times, which is the same data the swept-region reservation above needs. Because the synthetic benchmark used straight blocks, the X-14 gain from rectangles is extrapolated, not measured.

The gate's cost comes from the same place. With up to 6 halvings across 3 gates per tick, the expensive part is calling `placeAtSlot` for 400 men per halving, not reading cells. The measured read costs are 0.05–0.13 ms for 16k–32k men. Testing a candidate pose as a company or bin rectangle needs one transform per company per halving. Reading only the rim is valid under an invariant: the interior was clear at the last accepted pose and the motion is under one cell, so any newly blocked cell enters through the rim. The test must read every rim cell, not just the corners, because corners alone miss a one-cell-thick line crossing an edge. Per-man reads cannot be avoided where the gate keeps "newly blocked" semantics, but those semantics can move to per company or per bin.

### An order of work that reaches 2 ms

| Step | Effect | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Dedupe the halo per core cell; reuse the post-step claim at tick start | 4.64 → 3.06 ms | Measured; first exact, second one-tick over-claim after casualties |
| 2. Claim by bin or company rectangle, inflated for the margin, replacing the post-step per-man `claimFormation` | Rebuild about 0.1–0.2 ms instead of about 1 ms; step about 2.0–2.2 ms | Estimate |
| 3. Replace `claimsNear` with a 16 m coarse owner mask (about 0.005 ms to build) so companies with no foreign claim nearby get their terrain quick rejects back | Recovers part of the 0.45 ms | Build cost measured; recovery estimated |
| 4. Gate by rectangle rim for straight shapes | Helps ticks with many halvings; measure on X-05/06/08, not X-14 | Inference |
| 5. Replace the `more` flag with an owner count or overflow index; on maps of 1500² and larger, clear sparsely or keep a claims window around the troops | Fixes erase-by-owner and the three-owner block | Inference from the code |

The budget is tight. The baseline without claims is already 1.55 ms, which leaves the grid about 0.45 ms. Rectangle claims (about 0.1–0.2 ms) plus per-man queries (about 0.12 ms) fit inside that. Placing every man twice a tick does not fit, whatever else changes. WebAssembly and SIMD are unlikely to help. Surma measured tuned AssemblyScript at about 0.9x the speed of optimised JS on typed-array loops ([surma.dev](https://surma.dev/things/js-to-asc/)). The work here is short spans and branchy owner logic, and crossing the JS–Wasm boundary adds determinism risk. Each benchmark variant should run in its own process. Passing variants through one call site made it polymorphic and showed the unrolled `claim()` as 2x slower, when in separate processes it was 7–10% faster.

## Conclusion

The four problems share one fix. Company and bin rectangles are the right representation for the claim grid's cost, and the same rectangles serve the jam resolver's candidate poses, the swept-region reservation for formation changes, and the loser's backward step in melee. Building them first puts the budget and the behaviour fixes on one data structure. The grid's per-tick cost then depends on formations and companies (40 and about 320), not on men (16,000), which leaves headroom for the propose-and-grant pass. The diagnosis also changes what "deadlock" means here. Most of the observed stalls are goal contention and leader churn, which an arrival rule and a persistent priority remove. True wait-for cycles should be rare, and the wait-for graph is worth drawing as a debug overlay to confirm that.

The evidence is uneven. Jam handling rests on shipped code and theory with proofs (PIBT's reachability theorem assumes a biconnected graph, which open ground satisfies and defiles may not). The indivisible-act rules rest on tested tabletop practice and one robotics guarantee. The melee model has the least support. No source describes two deformable formations clashing front to front, and Pharaoh, the only game with continuous give-ground, does not publish how it works. The give-ground rate, the shear cap and the cohesion charged per denied step are therefore the parameters to settle in playtesting. Each tick should log which rule fired (granted, settled, inherited, refused, denied step), so replays can show why a battalion stopped.
